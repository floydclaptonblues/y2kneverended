Where did the Indian Ocean take the debris?

A forward-transport experiment using actual 2014–15 daily GLORYS currents finds abundant looping, southward excursions and westward reversals—but few particles reached the western Indian Ocean within the first year. This is a circulation result, not an MH370 crash-location inference.

The corrected finding: In the 35.5–38.7°S release band, 57 of 240 particles (6 h) and 68 of 240 (3 h) went south and later made a substantial westward excursion. An earlier endpoint flag incorrectly suggested that this never happened. Yet only 4 / 6 particles in that band crossed 80°E westward, and none of those western crossers completed the stipulated significant southward-then-westward sequence. The motion exists; the proposed delivery route was not established.

1. A strong eastward tendency, with rare western access

The modeled releases cover 33–45°S and 85–98°E, each with 12 initial positions within about 5 km of its cell center. They are a deliberately exploratory circulation survey, not a distribution of aircraft crash locations. The same 481 seeds were used in both timestep runs.

Across the full ensemble, 451 versus 452 particles crossed 100°E eastward. By contrast, 13 versus 11 reached 80°E westward, 4 versus 5 reached 70°E, and only 1 versus 2 reached 50°E. Crossing a longitude is not a beach arrival.

2. The western path mainly goes north before going west

Particles that did reach the western gates generally migrated equatorward, then entered westward transport along the subtropical gyre’s northern limb. This was not a persistent southern loop. The figure below shows the original simulated paths in both integrations, with the crossers highlighted. These are computed trajectories, not observed debris paths; their individual identities and timings are strongly sensitive to the timestep.

The southern cartwheel idea was therefore partly right about circulation, but was not established as a westward-delivery mechanism within this first-year window. The analysis found prolonged westward episodes, reversals and returns to earlier positions; an eddy-residence diagnostic alone does not establish a coherent material barrier.

The ending matters: the original chronology figure below shows that the 57 qualifying south-then-westward objects in the six-hour run began their westward episodes at a median of day 284; approximately 74% were still moving west over the last 30 days. Their journeys were truncated by the 365-day observation window. This does not establish whether those episodes would ultimately deliver debris westward or reverse east again.

3. The eastern boundary is the largest geographical limitation

The current dataset ends at 119.9167°E. At year-end, 235 particles in the 6 h run and 222 in the 3 h run had stopped because they left the data domain or entered masked/invalid velocity cells. Some masked cells lie near Australia's coast; they are not automatically beachings. For particles terminated at the artificial eastern edge, the model says nothing about their eventual return or destination.

The additional researcher-provided censoring map identifies one western-domain departure in the three-hour run, at approximately 45°E, 11.7°S. This is a modeled exit from the supplied current fields, not a verified landfall. The predominant termination pattern remains eastern, but the earlier blanket wording that every domain exit was eastward is too broad.

This is why zero recorded western crossings from a southern release group must not be read as a permanent exclusion: much of its population ceased being observed within the numerical domain before the end of the year.

4. What did the numerical refinement establish?

A full second year-long integration at a 3-hour step was completed using a verified checkpoint/restart procedure. The 80°E westward count changed from 13 to 11 and the 100°E eastward count from 451 to 452. This supports stability of the broad low-west/high-east population-level finding under the tested refinement, not demonstrated convergence to a unique limit.

Among 225 objects still active in both runs, corresponding year-end positions differed by a median of 846 km. Exact individual tracks and first-passage dates are therefore not numerically resolved. Path lengths and tortuosities also changed with timestep. It would be misleading to identify a physical debris route from any single simulated trajectory.

5. What this does—and does not—say about MH370

Debris eventually recovered on western Indian Ocean coasts is an observation-selected population. The actual recoveries depend on current transport, buoyancy, coastline encounters, detection and reporting; this simulation models none of those latter processes. It demonstrates how some starting locations can contribute to western pathways while many modeled objects travel elsewhere.

Interpretation boundaries: One historical GLORYS reanalysis, monthly-mean NCEP winds, an empirical 1.2% effective slip, no additional Stokes field, no diffusion or fragment-specific leeway, and no 508-day debris/beaching model. The geographic seeds are not posterior samples. G0 is a conditional sensitivity coordinate, not a verified crash contact. No seventh-arc likelihood, origin estimate, search recommendation or transport event horizon follows from these figures.
